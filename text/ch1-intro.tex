
\chapter{\label{ch:1-intro}Introduction} 

    \minitoc

    Sequential decision-making problems arise \mccorrect{whenever} an agent must choose a sequence of actions to achieve some goal, \mccorrect{where each action affects the environment, and hence the decisions that follow}. In games such as chess and Go, the agent's actions are moves on a board, and the goal is to win the game. In robotics, the agent's actions are motor commands, and the goal \mccorrect{may be to reach a target location without colliding with} obstacles. In drug treatment, the agent's actions are doses to administer, and the goal is to maximise the effectiveness of the treatment whilst minimising side effects.

    These problems are commonly modelled as Markov Decision Processes (MDPs), where an agent receives a reward signal at each timestep, and the goal is to maximise the cumulative reward over time. Reinforcement learning and planning algorithms have produced state-of-the-art results in such problems, and Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) is one of the most successful planning frameworks, famously underpinning the AlphaGo and AlphaZero programs that defeated the world's best Go players. 

    Many real-world problems involve more than one objective. The drug treatment problem mentioned above is multi-objective, with a trade-off between effectiveness and side effects. \mccorrect{In robotics, completing a task quickly may be desirable, but moving faster can increase the risk of damage to the robot or its surroundings.} Multi-objective Markov Decision Processes (MOMDPs) extend the MDP framework with a vector-valued reward function, and Multi-Objective Reinforcement Learning (MORL) extends reinforcement learning algorithms to operate in MOMDPs. \mccorrect{Preferences over the objectives are commonly captured by a vector of weights, one for each objective, together with a utility function. The utility function maps the weights and the vector-valued return to a scalar utility value.} When \mccorrect{the preferences are} not known at the time of planning, the agent must instead return a set of policies that cover the relevant trade-offs, \mccorrect{from which one is selected once the preferences become known}.

    This thesis is concerned with MCTS algorithms for sequential decision-making problems, in both the single- and multi-objective settings. The single-objective work in this thesis is motivated in part by the multi-objective work, and develops MCTS algorithms that are subsequently extended to the multi-objective setting. The remainder of this chapter \mccorrect{motivates the work, sets out its scope, states the research questions and contributions, describes the structure of the thesis, and lists the publications arising from it}.


\begin{mccorrection}
\section{Overview and Motivation}
\label{sec:1-1-overview}
\end{mccorrection}

    MCTS algorithms are sample-based planning algorithms that \mccorrect{often use a simulated environment.} The simulator is generally inexpensive to query, and a large number of trials can be run in the simulator before the agent must commit to actions in the real environment. The setting where the agent is evaluated only on the recommendations it makes after planning is \mccorrect{referred to in this thesis} as the \textit{exploration setting} of reinforcement learning. 

    \mccorrect{Because poor actions in a simulator incur little or no real-world cost, MCTS algorithms in the exploration setting can afford to explore more freely}. Despite this, the most widely used MCTS algorithm is the Upper Confidence Bound applied to Trees (UCT) algorithm, which uses a search policy designed for a different problem: minimising the cumulative regret of a multi-armed bandit. \mccorrect{In effect, UCT is designed for a setting where actions taken during planning do incur a real-world cost, and tends to select exploiting actions even when further exploration would be more valuable.}

    Maximum ENtropy Tree Search (MENTS) is a more recent MCTS algorithm that \mccorrect{addresses this limitation by using} an entropy regularised objective to encourage exploration. However, the maximum entropy objective is not guaranteed to align with the \mccorrect{standard objective of maximising expected return}, and the temperature parameter that governs \mccorrect{the trade-off between entropy and expected return is difficult to tune: it must be large enough to benefit exploration, but small enough to avoid misalignment}. A poor choice can result in MENTS recommending suboptimal actions. The first half of this thesis investigates how MCTS algorithms can prioritise exploration whilst still recommending optimal actions.

    The second half of this thesis is concerned with MCTS in the multi-objective setting. \mccorrect{Prior Multi-Objective Monte Carlo Tree Search (MOMCTS) algorithms have two limitations: they are restricted to deterministic environments, and they do not ensure that actions within a single trial are consistent with each other --- without such a mechanism, the search policy ends up optimising for no particular objective}. \mccorrect{To address these limitations,} this thesis introduces two MOMCTS frameworks for stochastic environments. \mccorrect{It is also argued that trials in MOMCTS should be \textit{contextual}: a single weight vector is sampled at the start of each trial and used to guide every action selection within it, so that the actions within a trial remain consistent}.



\begin{mccorrection}
\section{Scope of the Thesis}
\label{sec:1-2-scope}

    This section provides the scope and setting of the work completed for this thesis. Generally, the single-objective work (Chapter \ref{ch:4-dents}) considers the exploration setting described in Section \ref{sec:1-1-overview}, and the multi-objective work (Chapters \ref{ch:5-chmcts} and \ref{ch:6-simplexmaps}) considers the unknown weights scenario described below. 

    \paragraph{Simulation.} Throughout this thesis, the simulator is assumed to be an accurate model of the environment; model error, and how to compensate for it, are beyond the scope of this work.

    \paragraph{Planning and Execution.} In the \textit{planning phase}, the agent has access to a simulator of the environment and uses it to decide how to act. In the \textit{execution phase}, the agent acts in the environment itself. Running MCTS in the planning phase is called \textit{offline planning}: it is given a fixed compute budget before the agent must act. Running MCTS in the execution phase is called \textit{online planning}: it uses whatever time is available before each real-world decision to plan further from the current state using the simulator. Single-objective MCTS can be used in either phase, or both.

    \paragraph{Planning from a Known Initial State.} The methods in this thesis assume that the agent always begins in a known, deterministic initial state. A distribution over initial states could be handled by adding a pseudo-transition at the root of the search that samples the initial state, but this is not considered here: it amounts to several independent searches, unless transpositions (discussed in Section \ref{sec:2-4-2-thts}) are used to share nodes between them.

    \paragraph{The Unknown Weights Scenario.} The multi-objective reinforcement learning literature establishes several scenarios, distinguished by what is known about preferences and when. This thesis considers the \textit{unknown weights scenario} \cite{survey_mo_seq_dec_make,morl_survey}. Preferences over the objectives are specified by a weight vector $\bff{w}$, and a utility function maps a weight vector and a vector-valued return to a scalar utility. The weight vector is unknown during the planning phase, so the agent must plan for all possible preferences. It is revealed during the execution phase, after which the agent aims to maximise the utility for that weight vector. This setting arises whenever preferences are unavailable during planning, or vary between uses of a system.

    \paragraph{The Linear Utility Assumption.} The utility function is assumed to be linear in the weight vector throughout this thesis. Section \ref{sec:5-6-2-linear_utility} discusses why this assumption is made and how the work could be adapted to non-linear utilities. Section \ref{sec:5-6-3-fundamental_assumption_of_momcts} shows that MOMCTS algorithms that choose actions using the expected reward-to-go at each node yield a utility-optimal policy if and only if the utility is linear.

    \paragraph{Optimisation Criterion.} Two criteria are distinguished in the literature: the \textit{scalarised expected returns} (SER) criterion applies the utility to the expected return, whereas the \textit{expected scalarised returns} (ESR) criterion takes the expected utility of the return. SER is appropriate when a policy will be executed many times, so some poor individual executions can be tolerated. ESR is more appropriate when a policy is executed only once; for example, a patient may have only one opportunity to follow a treatment plan \cite{morl_survey,survey_mo_seq_dec_make}. This thesis uses the more widely studied SER criterion, and under the linear utility assumed here the two coincide. 

    \paragraph{Evaluation.} The algorithms in this thesis are evaluated as offline planners, although the single-objective algorithms of Chapter \ref{ch:4-dents} can equally be used for online planning. In the multi-objective unknown weights scenario, each action yields a vector of rewards, denoted $\bff{R}(s,a)$, with one component per objective. Once the weight vector $\bff{w}$ is revealed, the problem reduces to a single-objective one with scalarised reward $R(s,a;\bff{w}) = \bff{w}^\top \bff{R}(s,a)$, so any further online planning can be carried out by a single-objective MCTS algorithm. Hence, in this scenario, it makes little sense to use a MOMCTS algorithm for online planning. It follows that the quality of the solution computed during planning matters most at the initial state, where execution begins.

    \paragraph{The Role of Planning.} An offline planning budget could instead be spent learning a policy or value function that generalises across states, using function approximators such as neural networks. Why plan at all? MCTS algorithms can be shown to be consistent: given enough trials, they recommend an optimal action. On small environments a feasible budget may therefore be enough to recover an optimal decision, whereas a learned approximation carries no such guarantee. On larger environments the two approaches can be complementary: AlphaZero \cite{alpha_zero} uses a learned prior policy and value function to guide its search, and the results of that search to improve them in turn. The search methods developed here provide the foundation for such a combination, which is proposed as future work in Section \ref{sec:7-2-futurework}. Planning also has an incidental benefit not pursued here: a search tree can be inspected once planning is complete, which may aid interpretability. 
\end{mccorrection}



\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:1-3-contributions}

    The following questions, related to Monte Carlo Tree Search and multi-objective reinforcement learning, are considered throughout this thesis:
    \begin{enumerate}[start=1, label={\textbf{Q\arabic* -}}]
        % \item \hypertarget{q1}{\textbf{Time-Limited Planning:}} How do we ensure that MCTS algorithms make good decisions when limited planning time is available?
        \item \hypertarget{q1}{\textbf{Exploration:}} When planning in a simulator with limited time, how can Monte Carlo Tree Search algorithms prioritise exploration to make effective decisions?
        
        \begin{enumerate}[start=1, label={\textbf{Q1.\arabic* -}}]
            \item \hypertarget{q11}{\textbf{Entropy:}} \mccorrect{Entropy is commonly added to the objective in reinforcement learning to encourage exploration --- can this be done without compromising the optimality of the recommended actions?}
            \item \hypertarget{q12}{\textbf{Multi-Objective Exploration:}} Can the exploration insights from (maximum-entropy) single-objective Monte Carlo Tree Search algorithms be applied to multi-objective domains?
        \end{enumerate}

        \item \hypertarget{q2}{\textbf{Stochastic Multi-Objective Domains:}} Multi-objective Monte Carlo Tree Search has been studied in deterministic domains --- can these methods be extended to stochastic settings?

        \item \hypertarget{q3}{\textbf{Scalability:}} How can the scalability of Monte Carlo Tree Search methods be improved, in both single- and multi-objective settings?

        \begin{enumerate}[start=1, label={\textbf{Q3.\arabic* -}}]
            \item \hypertarget{q31}{\textbf{Complexity:}} Monte Carlo Tree Search algorithms typically run linearly in the number of trials, actions and decision horizon --- can this computational complexity be reduced?
            \item \hypertarget{q32}{\textbf{Multi-Objective Scalability:}} With respect to the size of environments, how scalable are multi-objective Monte Carlo Tree Search methods?
            % \item \hypertarget{q33}{\textbf{Curse of Dimensionality:}} With respect to the number of objectives, to what extent do multi-objective Monte Carlo Tree Search methods suffer from the curse of dimensionality?
        \end{enumerate}
    \end{enumerate}

    These questions are addressed by the following contributions:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Chapter \ref{ch:4-dents} investigates the use of entropy-based exploration in MCTS, addressing \entropyq. The maximum entropy objective is shown to be misaligned with the \mccorrect{standard objective of maximising expected return}: for any fixed temperature there is an MDP in which MENTS either fails to recommend the optimal policy, or requires an exponential number of trials to do so (Theorem \ref{thrm:ments_bad_mdp}). Two MCTS algorithms, \textit{Boltzmann Tree Search} (BTS) and \textit{Decaying ENtropy Tree Search} (DENTS), are introduced to address this misalignment, and are shown to be consistent under mild conditions. The stochastic search policies of BTS and DENTS also enable the use of the Alias Method to address \complexityq.

        \item Chapter \ref{ch:5-chmcts} addresses \stochq\ewe by introducing \textit{Convex Hull MCTS} (CHMCTS), which generalises prior MOMCTS work to stochastic environments by maintaining convex hull value sets at each node, and updating them via convex hull backups. \textit{Contextual Zooming for Trees} (CZT) is also introduced as a contextual analogue of UCT, in which the contextual zooming algorithm is applied at each decision node. %The notions of simple and cumulative regret are generalised to \textit{contextual simple regret} and \textit{contextual cumulative regret} for the multi-objective setting.

        \item Chapter \ref{ch:6-simplexmaps} addresses \contextq\ewe and \moscalabilityq\ewe by introducing the \textit{simplex map}, an adaptive data structure that maintains value and entropy estimates as a function of the weight vector over the unit simplex. Simplex maps address two limitations of the convex hull approach: the computational cost of convex hull backups in stochastic environments, and the inability to naturally support entropy-based exploration. The simplex map is used to define SM-BTS and SM-DENTS, which extend BTS and DENTS to the multi-objective setting.
    \end{itemize}



\section{Structure of Thesis}
\label{sec:1-4-thesis-structure}

    The remainder of this thesis is organised as follows. Chapter \ref{ch:2-background} introduces the background material used throughout the thesis. This includes the multi-armed bandit problem, Markov Decision Processes, reinforcement learning, the Monte Carlo Tree Search framework, and the multi-objective reinforcement learning setting. The \thtspp\ewe schema, used to define MCTS algorithms modularly throughout this thesis, is introduced in Section \ref{sec:2-4-2-thts}.

    Chapter \ref{ch:3-relatedwork} discusses related work in bandits, maximum entropy reinforcement learning, MCTS, and multi-objective sequential decision-making. The discussion is brief and focused on the references most relevant to the contributions of this thesis.

    Chapter \ref{ch:4-dents} considers MCTS algorithms for single-objective environments, with the optional use of entropy as a second objective for exploration. The maximum entropy objective is shown to \mccorrect{not always align} with the \mccorrect{standard objective of maximising expected return}, and BTS and DENTS are introduced as alternative MCTS algorithms. The stochastic search policies of BTS and DENTS also enable the use of the Alias Method, which significantly reduces the per-trial computational cost.

    Chapter \ref{ch:5-chmcts} extends the MCTS framework to the multi-objective \mccorrect{unknown weights scenario} with linear utilities, where the weight vector is unknown at the time of planning. CZT and CHMCTS are introduced as two approaches to contextual planning in MOMDPs. CHMCTS recovers prior MOMCTS algorithms as special cases of a common framework, and generalises them to stochastic environments.

    Chapter \ref{ch:6-simplexmaps} introduces simplex maps, an adaptive data structure for maintaining value and entropy estimates as a function of the weight vector. Simplex maps are used to define SM-BTS and SM-DENTS, which extend BTS and DENTS to the multi-objective setting, and are shown to scale better than CHMCTS in stochastic environments.

    Chapter \ref{ch:7-conclusion} summarises the contributions of this thesis and discusses possible directions for future work.
